The Story of Patan

One of the oldest cities in the Kathmandu Valley, Patan has been shaped by kingdoms, artisans, earthquakes, and the stubbornness of a community that kept building.

Ancient Origins

Kirat and Lichhavi Roots

Patan, originally called Lalitpur ("city of beauty"), has roots stretching back to the Kirat period and the early Lichhavi era. The city's foundations lie in the Kathmandu Valley's earliest settlements, where trade routes and Buddhist monasteries began shaping its identity long before the temples rose.

3rd Century BC

Ashoka's Four Stupas

Emperor Ashoka is credited with establishing the four stupas that still stand at the cardinal corners of Patan. These are among the oldest Buddhist monuments in Nepal and remain pilgrimage sites today, marking the city's sacred geography.

12th–18th Century

The Malla Golden Age

The Malla kings of Patan turned the city into a centre of Newar art, metalwork, woodcarving, and temple architecture. Durbar Square, Krishna Mandir, the Golden Temple, and countless courtyards were built or rebuilt during this era. Artisans worked in bronze, stone, and wood at a level that drew comparison with the finest cities in South Asia.

1769

Kingdoms End, City Remains

Prithvi Narayan Shah unified the valley's three kingdoms, ending Patan's political independence. But the city's cultural life — its festivals, food traditions, metalwork, and neighbourhood courtyards — continued largely uninterrupted.

1979

UNESCO World Heritage Listing

Patan Durbar Square was inscribed as part of the Kathmandu Valley UNESCO World Heritage Site, recognizing the extraordinary concentration of temples, palaces, and courtyards that make the city a living open-air museum.

2015 — Today

Earthquake and Living Restoration

The 2015 earthquake damaged many historic structures, including parts of Durbar Square. Restoration has since been carried out with a mix of traditional craft and modern engineering, proving that Patan's heritage is not frozen — it is actively rebuilt, maintained, and lived in by a community that has always treated the city as a work in progress.

Temples, palaces, courtyards, and courtyards behind courtyards

Places to Visit

Patan's monuments are not isolated attractions — they are woven into a city you walk through, eat in, and live around.

01

Patan Durbar Square

The royal palace complex and its surrounding temples form one of the most concentrated historic squares in Asia. Stone courtyards, carved windows, and temple facades open onto daily life — laundry lines, schoolchildren, and flower sellers.

UNESCO Heritage Site View on Google Maps
02

Krishna Mandir

Built entirely of stone in the 17th century, Krishna Mandir is one of the few shikhara-style temples in Nepal. Its carved friezes depict scenes from the Mahabharata and Ramayana. A generation of stonemasons worked on it before it was finished.

Stone Temple View on Google Maps
03

Patan Museum

Housed in the old royal palace, the museum displays bronze, stone, and wood sculptures from the Malla period. It is widely regarded as one of the finest museums of South Asian religious art, set in a restored courtyard.

Museum View on Google Maps
04

Golden Temple

Hiranya Varna Mahavihar, known as the Golden Temple, is a Buddhist monastery in the heart of Patan with a gilded roof and detailed metalwork. Monks and neighbours share the courtyard — a working religious space, not a museum.

Buddhist Monastery View on Google Maps
05

Mahaboudha Temple

Built with thousands of terracotta tiles inscribed with images of Buddha, Mahaboudha's form echoes North Indian temple design. Damaged in the 1934 and 2015 earthquakes, it has been rebuilt each time — a marker of Patan's approach to heritage as a living craft.

Terracotta Temple View on Google Maps
06

Ashoka Stupas

Four ancient stupas stand at the cardinal points of Patan — said to have been built by Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BC. They remain Buddhist pilgrimage sites and anchor the city's sacred geography.

Stupa View on Google Maps
Patan Durbar Square

A skyline carved, not built

Patan's Durbar Square was shaped over three centuries by Malla-era kings, artisans, and stonemasons whose names were rarely written down but whose work still stands. The stone Krishna Mandir alone took a generation to finish.

Walk far enough and the square gives way to quieter courtyards — Sundari Chowk's sunken royal bath, workshops where bronze is still cast by hand, and windows carved so finely they were once traded as gifts between kings.

Newar food, khaja sets, and festival feasts

Where to Eat in Patan

Patan's food scene is built on Newar culinary tradition — spiced lentils, beaten rice, bara, and yomari served in courtyards and family kitchens that have been cooking for generations.

Newari Home-Style

Honacha

Traditional Newari Restaurant

A well-known Patan institution serving authentic Newari khaja sets — beaten rice, spiced meat, lentils, pickles, and bara all on one platter. Locals and visitors alike come for the full traditional experience.

Courtyard Dining

Cafe Swotha

Newari Courtyard Cafe

Set in a restored Newari courtyard near Patan Durbar Square, Cafe Swotha serves traditional food alongside coffee and modern dishes. A calm place to sit above the square and watch the city.

Crafts, workshops, and living tradition

Culture That Never Stopped

In Patan, culture is not performed for visitors. Bronze casting, woodcarving, mask-making, and pottery continue in neighbourhood workshops the same way they did centuries ago — and they are open to anyone who walks in.

01

Watch the Makers

Look into workshop courtyards with curiosity, then ask before photographing or entering. A short conversation often reveals more than a signboard.

02

Follow the Courtyards

Take the smaller lanes between the square, Kumbheshwar, and the old bahals. They are where Patan’s everyday rhythm becomes visible.

03

Leave Room to Linger

Build time for a tea stop, a temple bell, or an unexpected procession. Patan rewards an unhurried walking pace.

A respectful first day

Plan Your Walk

Use the square as your compass, start early, and treat temples and neighbourhood courtyards as living places rather than backdrops.

  • MorningBegin at Patan Durbar Square while the light is soft and the museums are opening.
  • MiddayFollow the lanes to the Golden Temple and stop for a Newari lunch near Mangal Bazaar or Swotha.
  • AfternoonChoose one slower detour: Kumbheshwar, Mahaboudha, or the Ashoka Stupas.
  • RespectDress modestly in sacred places, remove shoes where requested, and ask before photographing people or rituals.
The chariot never stops

Rato Machindranath Jatra

Once a year, a towering wooden chariot is built by hand in Pulchowk and pulled through Patan's streets over several weeks — one of the longest-running chariot festivals in the world, drawing the entire valley into its path.

It's less an event to watch than a city to join: rope in hand, alongside neighbours who've done this for generations.

Festivals through the Nepali year

Festive Calendar

Patan celebrates more festivals per year than most cities have public holidays. Dates follow the Nepali lunar calendar and shift each year, but the sequence stays the same.

Chaitra – Jestha (Mar – Jun)

Rato Machindranath Jatra

One of the longest chariot festivals in South Asia. A towering wooden chariot is built by hand in Pulchowk and pulled through Patan's streets over several weeks. The entire city joins in — rope in hand.

Dates vary each year, lasting several weeks.
Chaitra – Baisakh (Mar – Apr)

Bisket Jatra Season

The Nepali New Year brings the erection of a ceremonial pole in Bhaktapur and chariot processions across the valley, including parts of Patan. A dramatic, noisy, community-wide celebration.

Marks the Nepali New Year.
Shravan (Jul – Aug)

Janai Purnima

A sacred thread-changing festival for Hindu men, followed by a day of special lentil soup (kwati) across the valley. In Patan, the festival sits alongside Buddhist observances, reflecting the city's mixed religious life.

Lunar date shifts each year.
Shravan – Bhadra (Aug – Sep)

Gai Jatra

A festival of humour and remembrance, marking those who died in the past year. Families send a cow (or a child dressed as one) through the streets, and the city fills with satire, performance, and laughter.

Particularly lively in Patan.
Bhadra – Ashwin (Aug – Sep)

Indra Jatra Season

Though most associated with Kathmandu, the festival's tantric and chariot traditions also appear in Patan. Mask dances and processions mark the honouring of Indra and the dead.

Linked to wider valley celebrations.
Ashwin – Kartik (Sep – Oct)

Dashain

Nepal's longest and most important festival. Families return home, elders bless children with tika, and kites fill the sky. In Patan, the festival adds extra life to already busy courtyards and family homes.

15 days, the biggest national holiday.
Kartik (Oct – Nov)

Tihar / Deepawali

The festival of lights. Lamps line every doorstep, dogs are garlanded, siblings celebrate each other, and buildings glow with rows of oil lamps. Patan's old city, with its dark courtyards and carved windows, is especially atmospheric during Tihar.

Five nights, city-wide illumination.
Mangsir (Nov – Dec)

Yomari Punhi

A Newari harvest festival centred on yomari — a steamed rice flour dumpling filled with molasses and sesame. The festival marks the end of the harvest season and is particularly celebrated in Patan's Newar households.

Unique to Newar culture.
